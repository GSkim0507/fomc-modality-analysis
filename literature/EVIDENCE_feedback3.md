# Evidence that central-bank text features explain and lead macro/market outcomes

Internal evidence memo for draft v2 (FOMC modal constructions). Sources: seven evidence dossiers (Kawamura et al. 2019; Hansen & McMahon 2015; Ehrmann & Talmi 2017; Gardner, Scotti & Vega 2021; Lucca & Trebbi 2009; Aruoba & Drechsel 2024; Byrne et al. 2021; Shapiro & Wilson 2021) plus the structured referee-caveat analysis (Blinder et al. 2008; Bholat et al. 2015; Kawamura et al. 2019; Ehrmann & Talmi 2017). Every number and page anchor below is carried from those dossiers; weak or ambiguous evidence is flagged as such.

## 1. Text leads macro (per-paper evidence)

| Citation | Design | Key numbers | Lead/lag verdict |

Text-cleaned shocks give theory-consistent IRFs (GDP↓, U↑ at ~1y; prices significant only after ~30 months) where Romer-Romer shocks are flat (Fig. 5, pp. 29–31). The mechanism — hedged risk narrative around a modal point forecast — is our motivating bridge, not a modal-verb result. |

| **Lucca & Trebbi (2009)**, "Measuring Central Bank Communication," NBER WP 15367. | FOMC statements May 1999–Dec 2008; automated semantic-orientation hawkishness scores (Google PMI-IR; Factiva antonym counts); univariate forecasting regressions of realized fed funds/Libor; Taylor-rule decomposition; recursive VARs. | Score changes forecast realized short rates with power **rising in horizon**: fed funds ~5 bp (1M) → ~25 bp (5M) per s.d.; Libor 4–70 bp out to ~2 years, peak ~55 bp at ~1⅔ years with term-premium controls (Tables 6–7, pp. 25–26). VAR: hawkish text shock ⇒ hump-shaped FFR response peaking **~30 bp about one year after** (Fig. 6, pp. 31–33). Score leads the Taylor-rule *residual* by 1–2 quarters (~70% correlations; Figs. 4–5, pp. 28–29). | **Shapiro & Wilson (2021)**, "Taking the Fed at its Word," FRBSF WP 2019-02. | LM net-negativity per speaker per meeting from FOMC transcripts (1,676 obs., 2000–11), regressed on *same-meeting* Greenbook forecasts and *trailing* financial variables; implicit π* backed out. | Loss-function estimates: implied π* ≈ 1.25–1.5 (e.g., \|π−π*\| coef 0.714*** (SE 0.148), π*=1.38 [1.16, 1.6]; Tables 1–2, pp. 40–41). Human-audit Spearman 0.40, p<0.0001 (p. 14, Fig. 1). | **CONTEMPORANEOUS/REACTIVE — not a lead.** Negativity is modeled as *responding* to same-meeting forecasts and trailing (90-day) market variables; no horse race shows it predicts future macro at any horizon. **Section 1 bottom line.** Two designs show a genuine text→macro lead with well-identified horizons: Kawamura et al. (polarity leads the leading index by ~3 months, Granger 1%) and Aruoba & Drechsel (pre-meeting text predicts the Fed's own 1–2-year-ahead forecast errors). Everything else leads *policy* or *expectations*, and the two direct tests of text vs realized macro beyond forecasts return nulls (Gardner et al. fn. 24; Lucca & Trebbi p. 31) or weakness (Hansen & McMahon real-variable IRFs). For modality specifically, the only direct evidence (Kawamura) is level-robust but **dies in first differences** (p. 241) — mark this weak.

## 3. What this licenses us to claim about modal constructions

Careful wording matters; the evidence supports three tiers, in decreasing strength.

**"Explain" — safe.** Modal/forward-looking text features *explain* market reception and encode macro state:
- Future-tagged (will/shall + infinitive) content more than doubles explained announcement-window yield news (0.28→0.63 adj. R², Byrne et al. Table 2) — the modal construction is demonstrably the vehicle of the projection channel.
- Modal/ambiguous expressions are significantly counter-cyclical (r up to −0.61 with the leading index) and retain explanatory power over the leading index controlling for VIX (Kawamura Table 8; adj. R² gain up to 0.30).
- Textual *change* per se explains volatility (−1.165***, +9% of log conditional variance), with a ~4× premium on boilerplate-breaking edits (Ehrmann & Talmi) — direct license for the boilerplate-edit-event framing.

**"Predict / lead" — claim narrowly, by object.** The defensible sentence is: *forward-looking modal register in central-bank text leads policy actions and market/forecaster expectations, and in some settings macro indicators.* Specifically:
- Leads **policy**: >1 year for the funds rate (Lucca & Trebbi, ~30 bp peak at ~1 yr; ~45% of FFR FEV at 6–12M); next meeting's decision (Gardner et al., pseudo R² 0.26; Ehrmann & Talmi Table 3).
- Leads **market conditions**: 25% of 10-yr-yield 1-month-ahead FEV (Hansen & McMahon); realized short rates up to ~2 years (Lucca & Trebbi), surviving post-announcement price controls.
- Leads **beliefs about macro**: Blue Chip GDP/unemployment/inflation revisions at 1–3-quarter horizons (Gardner et al. Table 6A).
- Leads **macro indicators** in two settings only: BoJ polarity → leading index (+3 months, Granger 1%) and Fed pre-meeting text → own 1–2-year forecast errors (Aruoba & Drechsel Table 2). Do NOT generalize to "modal constructions predict realized macro aggregates": the direct tests return nulls (Gardner fn. 24 — nothing beyond Blue Chip; Lucca & Trebbi — inflation/payroll IRFs ≈ 0; Hansen & McMahon — real effects weak), and the one modality-specific lead result weakens to insignificance in first differences (Kawamura p. 241).

**"Cause" — do not claim.** Nothing here identifies a causal effect of language on the economy. The consistent reading across papers (explicit in Kawamura pp. 231, 235) is that text leads because it *encodes the central bank's forecast*. Our claim should be framed as predictive/informational content — modal constructions as a legible carrier of the committee's forward-looking assessment — never as language moving the macroeconomy. The one near-causal market result (Lucca & Trebbi's survival of post-announcement price controls) licenses "the text carries information beyond what markets had priced," no more.

**Recommended formulation for draft v2:** modal constructions in FOMC statements are the grammatical vehicle of the forward-guidance register — the only register with demonstrated news content (EcSit null, H&M) — and text features of that register have been shown to explain market reception contemporaneously and to lead policy decisions, yields, and forecaster expectations at horizons from six weeks to two years; evidence that they lead *realized* macro aggregates is thinner and setting-specific, and we test it as an open question, not assert it.

## 4. Referee caveats and how our design answers them

From the structured caveat analysis (page anchors: Ehrmann & Talmi = ECB WP 2023 printed pages; Bholat et al. = CCBS Handbook 33; Blinder et al. = NBER WP 13932; Kawamura et al. = JEDC journal pages).

1. **Reverse causality / Fed information channel** (the central objection). The Fed writes after seeing data and staff forecasts (Romer & Romer 2000 forecast advantage, cited Blinder et al. p. 10); Kawamura et al. say outright that "causality is highly likely to go from business cycles to the Bank of Japan's communications" (p. 235) and read their own 3-month lead as forecasting power (p. 231). **Answer:** claim only predictive (Granger/leading-indicator) content, never causal effect; test *incremental* predictive power conditional on SEP medians / Greenbook-Tealbook forecasts, and say explicitly that a zero increment means the modal index is a forecast proxy.

2. **Boilerplate persistence inflates level correlations.** Statements are drafted from the previous one (Ehrmann & Talmi p. 3); FOMC mean similarity 0.65 vs BoC 0.44 (p. 12); identical sentences recur across issues (Kawamura p. 241). Two persistent series mechanically inflate r. **Answer:** report changes as well as levels; HAC/block-bootstrap inference; report AR(1) of the modal series; better, make persistence the object of study via the boilerplate-edit-event design (which Ehrmann & Talmi's 4× result independently motivates).

3. **Modality results die under differencing — a direct precedent.** Kawamura sec. 3.3.1: Δpolarity stays correlated with Δleading (r=0.22) but "neither the frequency of modal expressions nor the average length of the sentences is correlated with the leading index when their monthly changes are taken" (p. 241) — with n=207, more than our ~100 meetings. **Answer:** the differenced specification is a headline robustness table, not an appendix; if our series also fails, reframe as regime/level information or as discrete edit events.

4. **Drafting-regime and chair changes confound the series.** Text properties shift with editorial preference (Poloz's blank-page policy; "the drafting of the press releases has undergone substantial changes" while content stayed stable — Ehrmann & Talmi p. 3). The 2021 will→would shift could be Powell-era drafting, not macro signal. **Answer:** chair fixed effects / sample splits (Bernanke, Yellen, Powell); show the relationship replicates within chair regimes; dummy out known statement restructurings.

5. **Endogeneity of communication content and context.** "Communication may be endogenous... because of a sudden change in the economic outlook" (Blinder et al. p. 29); low-frequency identification is much harder than high-frequency (p. 27). **Answer:** FOMC statement *timing* is fixed in advance (mitigates timing endogeneity per Blinder p. 29); for content endogeneity, control for the contemporaneous macro state using real-time data vintages.

6. **Small sample, low power.** ~100 statements; the prior report's dot-plot correlation is r=−0.374 at n=39. Kawamura's power arithmetic (\|r\|>0.179/0.137 for 1%/5% at n=207, p. 235) implies far higher thresholds at n≈39–100, and single episodes (2008, 2020) can drive r. **Answer:** exact CIs; leave-one-episode-out (drop ZLB, drop COVID); prefer sentence/edit-level analysis (thousands of observations); genuine out-of-sample evaluation (train pre-2019, predict post-2019).

7. **Multiple testing and researcher degrees of freedom.** Kawamura Tables 5–6 report dozens of uncorrected correlations (pp. 235–236); dictionary construction is discretionary ("focus[es] only on words pre-judged by the researcher," Bholat et al. p. 2; "raw word counts can be misleading," p. 10); arbitrary MSI weights (will = would = 1.0) are a version of this. **Answer:** pre-specify a small set of primary hypotheses (e.g., will/would share vs next-quarter activity); FDR/Bonferroni on any correlation matrix; robustness across index definitions (share vs density vs per-document).

8. **Modal meaning is context-dependent — the sign can flip.** Kawamura: the same modal category correlates negatively with the leading index for current-state sentences but positively for future-state sentences (p. 236; high-prob flips from −0.37** to +0.60**); Bholat et al. p. 10 on negation/irony/ambiguity. English *would* is worse (conditional, past-habitual, reported-speech, politeness). **Answer:** split sentences by temporal reference and genre before counting; dependency-parse to flag conditionals and indirect quotation (Minutes' reported *would*); report per construction (modal + head verb), not pooled modal counts.

9. **The indicator needs external validation before any "leading" claim.** Ehrmann & Talmi validate tone against the bank's own next decisions first (ordered probit, pseudo-R² up to 0.12, p. 12); Bholat et al. stress held-out validation (p. 13). **Answer:** validate the modal index against near-term, well-identified outcomes first (next-meeting action, dot-plot direction, high-frequency release-window surprise — the Gardner et al. and Lucca & Trebbi templates), then test macro content; human-annotate a sample of modal instances and report precision/recall.

10. **Interacting text channels bias single-channel regressions.** Kawamura sec. 3.3.4: polarity and modality co-move in recessions and regressing one on the other "would yield biased estimates" (p. 241); a modal index may just proxy tone. **Answer:** always report the modal index's marginal contribution controlling for a standard tone measure (Loughran-McDonald — itself motivated by generic-dictionary failures, Bholat et al. p. 10); show the modal and tone series are not collinear, or model them jointly.
